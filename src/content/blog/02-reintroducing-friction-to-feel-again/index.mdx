An iPod Mini has been sitting on my desk for the last few days. It’s a small object from an era when your music had edges: limited storage, cables, syncing, a bit of waiting, and choices you had to live with.

It’s made me notice how little resistance there is in my day, in very ordinary ways.

Most mornings I make coffee with a pod machine. One button, done, and it’s fine. But recently I started setting aside time for a V60 pourover instead: boiling the water, grinding the beans, watching the bloom. It takes fifteen minutes I didn’t think I had, and every time the coffee tastes better than the pod, not only because of the method but because I was there for it.

I put on Spotify and let it decide what comes next, and it does a good enough job that I never intervene. YouTube plays in the background while I work, sliding from one video to the next without a pause long enough for me to ask whether I actually want to keep watching.

And then there’s the part that sits closer to my identity: I open a terminal, type `claude`, and a second later I’m moving again.

I’m conflicted about this, because I love how good these tools are. They’re impressive, they’re useful, and they lower the barrier in a way that still feels slightly unreal. In the last few weeks I built two small apps from nothing in a handful of days. Things I wanted for myself, with no roadmap and no meeting cadence. Just an idea, a tight feedback loop, and a lot of “try again”. In the moment it felt great in a very direct way: I had a thought, I tested it, something real appeared on my screen.

What I didn’t expect was how quickly “I shipped” turns into “okay, what now?”

I think part of it is that the whole stack used to push back more, not just the software. Hardware limits, storage, battery, bandwidth. You felt the constraints and they shaped what you even tried in the first place. A lot of that friction was pointless, but some of it was formative, because you had to sit with a problem long enough for it to change how you approached it.

When I started my career I joined a small agency called Siberia, back when the London studio was still young. I didn’t know anything about “shipping” as a concept, or what good client work was supposed to look like. I remember spending days on a single interaction: we were building an app where you’d snap a picture of your sneakers and they’d appear in a virtual wardrobe, model name and colorway pulled in automatically. Image recognition was still early, and I wanted the moment of capture to feel like magic.

So I sat with the animations. The way the shoe landed on screen, the timing of the label fading in. None of it was strictly necessary, but building each transition by hand meant you felt every decision, and you noticed what worked and what didn’t. The tech was part of it, but it wasn’t the point. The point was the thing we were trying to make real, and the constraints forced you to care about the whole shape of it.

The work changed over time. Bigger teams, more layers, less making and more coordinating, more hours in documents than in code. That’s fine, that’s often how a career goes. But with agents I can feel another shift, where coding looks less like the craft and more like a lever you pull to move ideas forward faster. In theory that should free me up for the parts that matter most: taste, decisions, trade-offs, clarity.

And yet I keep coming back to the same question. When everything is one click away, when every feature is one prompt away, what’s left for us to earn? Not earn as in deserve, more like: what’s left that asks something of us, that makes us choose and commit and then live with the choice.

I have a memory of being a kid and messing around with cheat codes. Sometimes I’d skip the hard part of a game, or trigger the ending without playing much of it, and it felt like seeing something I wasn’t supposed to see, like slipping backstage. A quick rush that evaporated almost immediately. I barely remember any of the games I finished that way. Coding with these tools sometimes gives me the same weird feeling of having achieved something without taking anything home from it. The games I do remember are the ones that asked me to stay and try again, and that built patience and attention without me noticing.

I’m not trying to make life harder for its own sake. But I do wonder whether a life that never pushes back slowly trains you to stop pushing.

So lately I’ve been experimenting with small, deliberate constraints. Nothing heroic. The iPod is one of them. Choosing an album instead of a playlist. Turning autoplay off. Writing down what I’m building and why before I open the terminal, so the tool doesn’t quietly decide the direction for me.

I’m not sure “friction” is even the right word for any of this. But when everything gets smoother I miss the moments that make me feel involved, and these are my attempts at getting a few of them back.
