Earlier this year I was telling my dad about my new scanner, a Nikon CoolScan IV ED I'd bought after my Plustek developed a weird defect and I sold it for parts. He let me go on about it for a while, and then said, completely casually: "you know when I was still in film, I used to develop my B&W photos myself, it really isn't complicated, you save a fair amount of money and you don't have to wait weeks anymore before sending to the lab".

A week later I was researching every chemical I'd need to start developing at home.

If only he knew what he had just started. He'd done this to me once before, with a camera he wasn't using.

That was 2009. I'd just finished high school and left my parents to live four hours away in Belfort, for my first year of what we call here in France "classes préparatoires", on the way to an engineering degree. My dad lent me his Nikon D70 (I don't think he was really using it anymore, he'd switched to a much smaller digicam by then), and I took photos with it once in a while, mostly our daily life with my roommate. Nothing serious. But I was learning the basics, and enjoying it far more than I expected to.

After miserably (luckily?) failing that first year, I was back in Paris at my parents' house, studying applied electronics from 2010. It was a lot more chill, even though I had to catch up on quite a lot of stuff I'd never studied before, and it left me with enough time to have a hobby again. This time I wanted one that was mine.

After scouring the second hand market in Paris, I spotted a mint Nikon D1x for 150 euros. It was already a ten year old camera back then, but still very decent knowing it had been top of the line when it was released in 2001. With Adobe Lightroom you could unlock the full 10MP of the sensor when shooting raw, which felt like plenty for me.

Then I bought a brand new Nikon AF-S 50mm f1.4 G for near 300 euros, twice what I'd just paid for the camera. It didn't strike me as strange at the time. A Nikon 80-200mm f2.8 AF followed a few months later, because I was craving the reach and wanted to try portraits. Then a 28mm f2.8 AI-S, cheap because of a little scratch on the front element that was completely invisible in pictures. Every summer job I did between 2010 and 2012 converted more or less directly into lenses.

I fell in love with the D1x instantly. It's a big, heavy camera, mostly based on the F5's design released a few years before it, but god, every control is right under your fingertips exactly when you need it. You somehow become one with the body, and it all makes sense.

I started going out to Paris nearly every weekend to take photos. At first it was just me on my own in parks and touristy places, and then, as I started sharing some of my photos on Facebook, my friends happily played model for me to practice on.

I remember one afternoon in a park near the Bibliothèque François Mitterrand, which back then was our HQ with my close friends. There was a small waterfall with a bench sitting right in front of it, perpendicular to the water, and something clicked. Is this what a good composition looks like?

I took a first frame and liked it, but on the little screen it was nothing special. Nowhere near as good as what I could see in front of me. Then I remembered reading that to get that soft, steady flow you need a longer exposure. So I slowed the shutter right down, took the shot, and everything came out blindingly bright. I closed the aperture a few stops, tried again, and there it was, the water exactly how I'd pictured it in my head.

In 2012 I was lucky enough to go to Vietnam for three months for an internship, and I brought the D1x with me. I absolutely loved taking photos there. It was my first time shooting seriously somewhere that wasn't home.

And then it stopped.

In 2013 I moved to Troyes to study computer science for another three years, which would give me yet another degree to reach the master's level we were being told would "unlock all doors" and give us the job we were dreaming of. I can't really remember why I didn't bring the D1x with me. I spent three years there and I can barely remember taking any photos at all. My best guess is that this is exactly when iPhone cameras got really good, and I was just happy with what I was getting out of it.

I never decided to stop. The phone was always there, it was good enough, and at some point I stopped noticing that I wasn't really taking pictures anymore.

I graduated in summer 2015 and quickly landed a job in London, where I very soon met who would become my wife. Not even a month after meeting each other we decided to go on a weekend trip to Morocco. I knew I wanted photos of us traveling, and the phone in my pocket suddenly wasn't going to cut it, so a few days before flying I decided I needed a camera. After looking at the second hand market in London, and reading about these new mirrorless bodies, I found a Fujifilm X-E2 for a good price that came with the XF27mm f2.8. Perfect. I met the guy literally the night before flying, at the exit of a metro station, kinda strange and scary at the same time. I fired the shutter a couple of times, it sounded like a shutter, I handed him the cash. I got on the plane with a camera I had no idea how to operate.

In Morocco I fell in love with photography again. Mirrorless meant a lot less faff, it was super quiet, and this body was the lightest one I've ever touched. I rediscovered how much more aware a camera makes me in a new place: I was looking left to right, up and down, the whole time. Somewhere in that batch are the first photos I ever took of her. In one of them I'm following her through the souks of Marrakech, her blond hair catching the sunlight coming through the ceiling.

The X-E2 came along on nearly every trip we took from then on. I bought an adapter to keep using my F lenses, and around 2018, when 7Artisans started making lenses for the X mount, I got their inexpensive but great 35mm f2, an all metal manual lens. I really enjoyed the pancake style.

Then it faded again. We moved to France in 2021, and the X-E2 had only ever really been a travel camera. For a while there were no trips to take it on, and it stayed in its bag, and the longer it sat there the less I thought about reaching for it.

Winter 2024, we started planning Japan. I had always dreamed of going, growing up as a kid in the 90s, and I knew I wanted to bring back something _special_. Around the same time, for reasons I still can't fully explain, I felt like taking photos again. I kept thinking about the old-school side of Japan that people manage to capture so beautifully in black and white. And if I wanted B&W, and something I could actually hold at the end of it, there was a fairly obvious answer.

So there I was, googling my way into the rabbit hole that is film photography. I had a lot of F mount glass, so of course I looked into an old Nikon body. The general consensus on ~the internet~ Reddit seemed to be that the Nikon FE is a good entry point. Cheap enough that I wouldn't break the bank, and it had the old "tactile" experience I was looking for. I also wanted something that would slow me down and have me enjoy the trip more.

I got a Nikon FE from a guy not far from where I live, with absolutely no way to test it other than firing it empty, but he said he had used it in the past and it worked great, so I trusted him. A week later, and just a few days before leaving, I found a Nikon F90x online for 40 euros. By that point I'd spent enough hours on Reddit to know it was a good model too, and that I could use my AF lenses with it, so I bought it. It arrived two days before we left. I found the body so cool that I couldn't not bring it with me. It was sold as-is, and all I knew was that it turned on and seemed to react to the different shutter speeds more or less accordingly.

I got on the plane with four rolls of HP5 Plus and a forty euro camera I wasn't sure worked.

I already had quite a few years of practice behind me at that stage, so I knew my way around the controls. Loading film was the part I'd never done: leader out to the little red mark, close the back, listen to it wind itself to frame one. The only other real new thing was that you can't change the ISO mid roll. If I wanted to shoot at night I had to crank it to 1600 (HP5 Plus handles this so well, it's insane) and that would be my night-time roll until it was finished.

At the Iwatayama monkey park in Arashiyama, I didn't have to wait long for the monkeys to do something silly, and I was there to frame it. It isn't a great photograph. I had thirty-six frames on that roll and I spent one of them on it.

I had an _amazing_ time shooting with the F90x. So much so that I bought another pack of three UltraMax 400 in Tokyo. I was hooked.

I got the rolls developed when we got back and I was overwhelmed with the results. I loved the grain, the colours, the fact that it isn't perfect.

I quickly bought a Plustek scanner, because I knew scanning costs would very likely be my first blocker. I found a good deal at 200-ish euros and figured that if my lab charges 24 euros per roll to scan, I'd break even around eight rolls, and I'd come back from two weeks in Japan with seven. It made sense.

This is where the rabbit hole gets deeper. Scanning is its own set of decisions: how much contrast, how much grain, how you want the thing to look.

About twenty rolls later, in 2026, for the first time ever I've taken more photos with my film cameras than with my digital one.

Which brings me back to my dad.

Developing at home was scary the first time, and addictive right after. It's slower than digital, more complicated, more expensive. My rolls just weren't sitting at a lab for weeks anymore. I shot close to ten of them in about two months, more than I'd have taken on my digital body in the same time.

Pulling the negatives out of the tank that first time (you load the reel by feel, hands inside a changing bag, in total darkness), seeing all those frames sitting there on the strip, I got an adrenaline rush I hadn't felt in years. Like I'd worked something out entirely on my own. Which, of course, my dad had worked out for himself more than forty years ago.

When I told him about it, his first reply was: are you going to print them next?

Oh boy.

I brushed it off at first. Printing is too much faff, I'd need a darkroom, I'd need this and that.

A few weeks later I was visiting a guy's studio near where I live, originally just to buy expired film off him. He had a dozen enlargers sitting there ready to use, plus what looked like forty years of other people's darkrooms washed up in one room: trays, easels, boxes of paper from brands that no longer exist. He spends most of his free time scouting nearby yard sales for photography gear and had built up quite a collection over the years, and when he saw how interested I was, he offered me one at a good price.

A week later I made my first print at home.

Setting up the enlarger, barely touching the focus knob to find the grain through the focuser, running test strips to work out the contrast, and then watching the print appear in the developer bath. Under the red light the paper stays blank for a few seconds, and then our dog Leo's face comes up out of the white, a little darker every time you rock the tray. In the tank you never see any of it happen. I'm barely scratching the surface. Since then I've mostly been reading about dodging and burning, and practicing.

It isn't cheap. At this stage it's probably more expensive than the film itself. But people spend thousands a year on the things they love, and I've stopped trying to justify it on those terms.

The photo at the top of this post is a print I made myself, from a negative I developed myself.

I'm about to become a dad myself. I have no idea whether any of this will mean anything to my child, and I don't think that's something you get to decide for someone else anyway. But there will be a camera in the house that nobody is really using, and at some point I'll leave it somewhere they can find it.
