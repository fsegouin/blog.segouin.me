Here’s a thought I haven’t been able to shake lately: I don’t really own anything anymore.

The movies I watch live on streaming platforms. The music I listen to is tied to a subscription. I take thousands of photos every year and most of them end up somewhere in the cloud, never looked at again. Everything is accessible, and almost none of it feels like it’s actually mine.

This isn’t a complaint about money. Things are better now in a lot of ways: access is easier, storage is cheap, and I wouldn’t seriously argue that we should go back. But something about it has been bothering me, and it took me a while to work out what.

When I think back to being a kid, what I remember is how attached I got to the very few things I actually had. One Christmas I was given a PlayStation 1 with two games, Crash Bandicoot and Spyro, and that was my entire world for a very long time.

I played them constantly. I replayed levels I’d already finished just to see if I could do them better, and I tried to collect everything even when it didn’t matter at all. I remember getting properly stuck on certain sections and slowly figuring them out through trial and error. It wasn’t always fun. Sometimes it was just frustrating. But I kept going, and it never once felt limiting, because I wasn’t thinking about what else I could have been playing. I had those two games and they were enough.

Looking back, I can see how much that shaped me. Those games rewarded exploration and didn’t always point you in the right direction, so you had to pay attention, experiment, and fail before you got any better. Somewhere in there I learned that I enjoy solving difficult problems, that I like chasing things to completion, and that I’m fine not being told exactly where to go next. The repetition wasn’t boring, it was building something.

I didn’t realise at the time how much ownership, even of something as small as a game, encouraged that kind of depth. When you only have a few things, you stay with them longer, and you end up in a relationship with them.

That thought sat at the back of my mind for years, and it only really came into focus during a trip to Japan in November 2024.

I’d wanted to go since I was a kid. Growing up in the 90s, Japan felt mythical to me, and when my wife and I finally planned the trip I knew I wanted to experience it differently, not entirely through my phone screen. So I bought a film camera.

Not a carefully tested one, either: an untested Nikon F90x I found for cheap and sold as-is. There was a real chance I’d come home with ten days of blank film, which made me slightly uneasy and, oddly, a lot more committed. I packed four rolls of HP5 Plus and decided that would be enough. A fixed number of exposures, no instant feedback, no second chances.

One evening in Tokyo I stood for nearly thirty minutes in front of a small shop in a quiet alley. The light was perfect and I could already picture how it would look in black and white. I waited for someone to walk through the frame to give the scene a bit of movement, and I remember hesitating before pressing the shutter, working out whether this moment was worth one of the exposures I had left.

That hesitation is the part I keep coming back to. Over those two weeks I barely touched my phone for photos. I couldn’t shoot twenty versions of something and sort it out later, so I had to decide in the moment whether it mattered, and that changed what I actually saw. Every frame was a decision rather than a recording. I’m fairly sure I’d have felt the same way even if every roll had come back blank, because the value wasn’t only in the images, it was in the way the process made me look at what was around me.

That trip made me think a lot more about how I consume everything else now.

So much of it runs without me. When I open a music app there’s already something waiting. When a show ends the next one starts without asking. Even when I take a photo, my phone suggests how it could be improved. None of this is bad, and most of the time it’s helpful, but I’ve noticed how easy it is to drift along with it. I don’t always choose what to watch next. I rarely scroll far past what’s recommended. I let it keep going because that’s easier than deciding.

I’m not claiming the 90s were better, and I don’t actually want to give up the convenience. But I do miss certain kinds of slowness, and I miss being bored. Boredom forced me to pick something, and those small choices, repeated over years, shaped my taste in ways I didn’t understand at the time.

We’ve gone from selecting and owning to accessing and being guided, and for a lot of people that reads as freedom: fewer decisions, less effort. I understand the appeal. But I’m starting to see what it costs. When I don’t actively choose something, I don’t build the same connection to it, and when nothing asks anything of me, I find it much harder to give it my full attention.

Japan didn’t need a film camera to be meaningful. It already was. The camera just slowed me down enough to feel it properly.

Since then I’ve been paying closer attention to how much of my day runs on autopilot, and I’ve started putting a bit of friction back into the places that seem worth it. Not everywhere. Just where it changes what I get out of something.
